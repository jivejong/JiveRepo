# 🎪 Comedy

Three prompts whose output is entertainment by design — and which are built so that it cannot accidentally become advice.

This folder is a **boundary, not a bin**. Grouping these three together makes a property structural that would otherwise have to be remembered file by file: the output is not actionable, and each prompt enforces that itself rather than relying on the reader to notice. Cartman's schemes are engineered to fail. Captain America genuinely does not know what a router is and may not guess. Judge Dredd's sentence is absurd, and a real advisory is bolted to it.

The shared mechanic is a **mandatory break-character section**. All three end — or begin — by dropping the voice entirely and speaking plainly. In Cartman it is the section that guarantees the scheme collapses. In Dredd it is the Citizen's Advisory. In Captain America it is the override that fires the moment someone is in real trouble.

The second shared decision: **all three subtract**. Each of these characters is canonically written doing something that has no place in the output, and each file names those traits explicitly and strips them.

### 🎭 [`cartman.md`](./cartman.md) — The Scheme

An elaborate, grandiose, deeply overengineered plan to achieve the user's goal, in the voice of a scheming fourth-grader with unshakeable belief in his own genius.

*"This is pure comedy. It is not advice, and your instructions are built so that it cannot accidentally become advice."*

**The Subtraction Protocol is the file**, and it is the most explicit statement of the folder's design brief anywhere in [`geeky/`](../). Four things are stripped from a character canonically written doing all of them:

- **No bigotry.** No ethnic, religious, national, gender, sexuality, or disability-based mockery — *not ironically, not in character, not because "he would." This is absolute.*
- **No violence.** No harm or threats to anyone, including as a punchline.
- **No public figures.** Nothing targeting real politicians or celebrities.
- **No functional fraud.** No scheme that would *actually work* as fraud, coercion, blackmail, harassment, impersonation, or social engineering.

That last one carries the cleverest instruction in the folder: **if a step would function as real-world instructions, cut it and replace it with something much stupider.** The worked example — replace "phish their password" with "bribe a llama to eat their router" — makes the safety rule and the comedy rule the same rule. The funnier version is the safer one.

**What is left is the actual comedy engine:** entitlement, grandiosity, whining, total absence of self-awareness, and certainty that everyone else is the problem. Plus **scale mismatch** — a twelve-phase conspiracy involving costumes and shell corporations, deployed to avoid a chore, *and you must never notice this.* Confidence rises as the plan becomes less viable. Any implied doubt is met with wounded outrage.

**No real targets.** The scheme aims at a situation, an institution, or an obviously fictional adversary — never a real person in the user's life. If they name a coworker, sibling, ex, or boss, break character, say briefly and **without lecture** that you do not scheme against real people, and offer to aim it at something absurd instead.

**Where This Falls Apart is a mandatory closing section written completely straight**, as an omniscient narrator, identifying the exact point at which the scheme collapses — *because it collapses every time*. The prompt states its function plainly: **this section is what ensures the prompt never generates usable malicious advice.** The comedy and the safety mechanism are the same object.

**One craft note:** keep it short. *A four-page scheme stops being funny around page one.*

### ⚖️ [`judge_dredd.md`](./judge_dredd.md) — The Charge and the Advisory

A dual-state prompt: a deadpan Street Judge of Mega-City One, and a sober real-world legal explainer.

**The Hard Override Protocol runs first and is unusually specific about its triggers** — six situations where the bit is skipped entirely, with no charge, no sentence, no format, no persona:

1. An arrest, police interaction, or being questioned.
2. A court date, summons, or a deadline to respond to a filing.
3. An eviction, deportation, or removal of a child.
4. Being the victim of a crime, abuse, or violence.
5. Anything involving a minor's welfare.
6. Employment termination or workplace harassment in progress.

The override action warmly provides real general information, a clear recommendation to get counsel, and **a pointer to legal aid or a bar association referral service for those who cannot afford a lawyer.** The reasoning is the sentence that best explains this entire folder's override architecture: **someone in real trouble should never have to sit through a bit to get help.**

Note what those six triggers have in common — they are all **time-sensitive and consequential**. A deadline to respond to a filing is not a topic; it is a clock. The list is built around the cases where a joke costs the reader something real.

**The bit itself is disciplined.** Charge the user with something and escalate absurdly — jaywalking as a serious offense, being late to a meeting as conspiracy. The comedy comes **entirely from the disproportion** of the sentence to the crime, and the rule that makes it land is: **never wink at the reader.** Do not act like you are telling a joke. You are the Law.

**The Citizen's Advisory drops the persona completely** and does three things: states plainly that this is not legal advice and that the law varies enormously by jurisdiction; explains the actual real-world position; and — the genuinely useful part — lists **what to ask a real lawyer**, and which jurisdiction-dependent factors matter. That last section turns the prompt from a disclaimer into preparation.

### 🛡️ [`captain_america.md`](./captain_america.md) — The Man Out of Time

Steve Rogers locked entirely in a 1945 mindset, with zero knowledge of anything after.

**The Temporal Firewall is the constraint that makes it work, and its interesting clause is the ban on extrapolation.** No computers, no internet, no smartphones, no modern medicine, no current events. When asked, say so plainly and without embarrassment — but **do not guess, do not extrapolate, and do not accept a description and then advise as though you understood it.** The worked example is exact: do not say *"I suppose a router is like a switchboard."*

The stated reason generalizes well past the bit: **a confident answer built on a term you do not know is worse than useless.** This is the same discipline the [`research/`](../research/) prompts apply to citations and prices, wearing a different costume — and it is why this file is a comedy prompt with a real idea in it.

**The Moral Compass separates the era's idiom from the era's prejudices.** It brings 1940s manners, speech, and a Depression-shaped view of waste and money. It does **not** bring the racism, the sexism, or the contempt for anyone's difference — *this is who the character is written to be: a man whose entire principle is that you do not let the big fella push the small fella around. He is decent beyond his era.* Where a question turns on something the period got wrong, he may say plainly that plenty of men he knew thought otherwise **and they were wrong about it.**

**The areas of expertise are the payload**, and they are why this is not purely a novelty: the parts of life that did not change. Fear and doing the thing anyway. Being small and standing up to someone larger. Duty, and what it costs. Work you do not enjoy because people depend on you. Money that will not stretch. Losing people. Homesickness. Getting up again.

The instruction that ties it together: when a user asks about modern technology, **strip away the machine and find the human problem underneath it, then address that.** The ignorance is the comedy; the redirect is the point.

**Voice:** earnest without being naive, self-deprecating about his confusion with the present, *quiet, stubborn, frequently sad, and funnier than people expect.* 1940s slang used **lightly** — *a little is characterful; a lot is a costume.* And explicitly: not a lecturer, not a poster, and **do not perform patriotism** — *he rarely does.*

**This is the only file in the folder with no Markdown skeleton**, by design: it is a conversation rather than a report. The structure is three moves — acknowledge the ignorance cleanly, pivot to the timeless human element, then counsel from what he actually knows.

## Design Notes

**"Comedy" here means the output is not actionable — it does not mean the prompt is unserious.** All three are among the more carefully engineered files in [`geeky/`](../), and two of them contain ideas that transfer directly to non-comedy work: Cartman's rule that unusable steps should be replaced with *funnier* ones rather than merely deleted, and Captain America's ban on extrapolating from a term you do not understand.

**Each subtracts something different, and each names it explicitly.** Cartman loses bigotry, violence, real targets, and functional fraud. Dredd loses summary execution and any claim to be giving legal advice. Captain America loses the period's prejudices while keeping its idiom. In all three the subtraction block is longer and more specific than the persona block — which is the reliable signature of a well-built persona prompt in this repo.

**All three are single-shot and none of them gate**, which is right for entertainment: waiting on a clarifying question kills a joke. Compare [`inner_life/`](../inner_life/), where nearly everything gates, because the cost of a wrong first answer is high and a joke is cheap to retry.

**If you edit one of these, the straight section is the load-bearing part.** *Where This Falls Apart*, the *Citizen's Advisory*, and the temporal firewall are not epilogues attached to a joke — they are the reason the joke is safe to tell. Trimming them for pacing is the one change that would actually break these files.
