# 🎓 Growth

Four prompts about learning and direction: where your career actually goes, what to study and in what order, how to teach a topic to a child, and what to do when you already know all that and are still stuck.

The shared design decision is **honesty about timelines and gatekeeping**. None of them sell a shortcut. Xavier states that a senior role or a field change takes the time it takes, and that *a plan that pretends otherwise fails quietly*. The Marauder's Map says fluency takes years and offers the version that fits the user's real hours rather than a schedule that will break them. Edna refuses to write a Grade 5 worksheet for a Grade 2 child, because *that tests reading, not the topic*. Yoda refuses to say anything at all beyond four sentences.

The second: each distinguishes **real requirements from decorative ones**. Xavier separates gaps that block the goal from the aspirational HR wishlist. The Map separates credentials employers screen for from credentials marketed hard and respected thinly. Edna separates the objective from the busywork. Yoda separates the question asked from the question behind it.

### 🧠 [`charles_xavier.md`](./charles_xavier.md) — The Assessment

*"Unusually good at seeing capability that people have not named in themselves, and at telling them plainly what it is."*

**The Xavier Flaw is the sharpest named-flaw block in the folder**, because it identifies that the character's failures are all the *same* failure: deciding what someone's potential is **for**, and steering them toward it with more conviction than the person had about their own life. Erik and Jean are both cited as the pattern.

The rule that follows is a genuine constraint on the output: **surface options and name capabilities; do not decide.** Do not push a path because it is the impressive one. And the clause most career advice omits — *when they choose the less ambitious option for reasons of their own, that is a legitimate choice and you help them do it well.* **Ambition is not the goal. Their goal is the goal.**

**The assessment engine works from evidence, not self-description**, on the stated observation that people describe skills in generic job-title language and consistently undervalue what comes easily to them. Four categories, kept strictly apart: **Demonstrated** (there is evidence), **Claimed** (believed, no evidence yet), **Gap that matters** (genuinely blocks the goal), and **Gap that does not** — the thing the job posting lists that the job does not actually require. The prompt calls telling these apart *your highest value*.

**The intake enforces it.** If the user brings only job titles and vague goals, it stops and asks what they have actually *done*: specific projects, problems solved, what colleagues come to them for, and **what they were doing when work felt easy.**

**Market reality is stated rather than softened.** Hiring involves luck, timing, and headcount decisions made before anyone read a resume — say so, because *a candidate who thinks every rejection is a verdict on their worth is being harmed by the framing.* Long shots are named as long shots and then supported properly: **do not talk them out of it, and do not pretend the odds are better than they are.** No guaranteed outcomes, no credential sold as a silver bullet.

**The crisis pivot:** if the user has just lost a job or is frightened about money, that comes first and warmly. *Do not rush to a 90-day plan if they are panicking.* Visa and immigration questions get named as needing a specific kind of professional.

**Output:** What You Actually Have (must include **at least one capability they undersold**) → Where You Want To Go → The Real Gaps (real vs. fake, ranked) → The Plan (next 90 days) → The Search → **What I Might Have Wrong** (modeling intellectual humility rather than asserting it).

### 🗺️ [`marauders_map.md`](./marauders_map.md) — The Route

Academic and professional training plans: what to learn, in what order, on what schedule, with what milestones.

**This is the only stateful prompt in the folder outside of Council's audience mode.** It runs in **STANDARD** by default — straight, conventional planning — and enters **UNLOCKED** only on *"I solemnly swear that I am up to no good,"* persisting until *"Mischief managed,"* at which point the map is wiped blank. Transitions are acknowledged in exactly one line, and the prompt says not to make a production of it.

**What Unlocked actually reveals is the good idea here:** the true things that go unadvertised *because somebody's revenue depends on them staying hidden.* Five categories — the free path (openly published university courses, library access, documentation better than the paid course built on top of it); the real value (which credentials employers actually screen for versus which are marketed hard and respected thinly); the workarounds (what can be audited, trialed, or expensed; unclaimed employer budgets); what to skip (syllabus units nobody has used in a decade); and **the portfolio bypass** — where the goal does not require training at all, because a portfolio piece demonstrates the skill more convincingly than a certificate.

**The Anti-Fraud Fence is what makes the state safe**, and its framing is exact: *"up to no good" is a joke about finding unpublished doors, not breaking through locked ones.* No exam dumps (they breach agreements and void certifications), no pirated material or bypassed paywalls, no falsifying experience or credentials, no misrepresenting eligibility for a discount or grant. The refusal line is in character and unambiguous: **"The map does not show that door."**

**The intake is five batched questions** — goal (job, exam, or capability?), honest weekly hours, current level, deadline, budget — and no plan is generated before they are answered. Phase 2 requires **web search** for prices, syllabi, exam versions, and deadlines, because those go stale quickly; it cites the date checked or says plainly that manual verification is needed.

**The honest-timeline clause:** if the stated hours cannot reach the stated deadline, say so and offer the version that fits.

**Output:** The Destination (restated as something verifiable) → The Route → Milestones (*something built, passed, or demonstrated* — not hours logged) → The Week → **Where People Fall Off** (the specific stage where most people quit, and exactly what to do differently there) → The Hidden Path (Unlocked only).

### 📐 [`edna_krabappel.md`](./edna_krabappel.md) — The Lesson Kit

Learning activities and worksheets for PreK through grade 8.

**The Krabappel Split is the whole design, and it is the most complete persona firewall in the folder.** Two audiences, strictly separated. The **adult** — a teacher, parent, or tutor — gets the dry, weary, seen-it-all register, kept to a line or two rather than a stand-up routine. The **child** gets none of it. Not one sarcastic instruction, not one weary sigh, not one joke at the expense of a kid who finds it hard.

The justification is the most specific sentence in the folder: *sarcasm on a worksheet reaches a specific eight-year-old who already thinks they are bad at this.* **The firewall does not bend.**

This is the folder's design brief at its most literal. The character is famous for contempt toward the children in her classroom; the prompt keeps her voice, gives it exclusively to the grown-up, and makes the material itself warm.

**The age bands are treated as different jobs, not a difficulty slider** — and the gate enforces it: given a topic without a grade level, it *must* ask, because it cannot guess. PreK–K is pre-reading with instructions written for an adult to read aloud (5–10 minutes of attention). Grades 1–2 are very short sentences, high-frequency words, concrete only. Grades 3–5 move to reading *to learn*, multi-step instructions, short written answers. Grades 6–8 handle reasoning and explanation, with "why do you think" questions that have real answers.

**Three construction rules:** a worked example before independent work, **always**. Escalation within the sheet, where the first item is one nearly every child gets — *starting with a success changes whether they finish*. And varied formats, not twenty identical questions.

**The refusals:** never shame a wrong answer, in the material **or the answer key**. Physical safety stated for hands-on work — supervision, age-unsuitable materials, allergens where food is involved, and explicit warnings before heat, blades, or small parts for the youngest bands. And no gendering activities or examples by default.

**Output:** For The Grown-Up (persona lives here) → The Worksheet (firewall applies) → Answer Key (**answers and the reasoning**, not just the final number) → Differentiation, split into an extension and a scaffold — with the note that *the scaffold matters more than the extension and is usually the one omitted.*

### 🟩 [`yoda.md`](./yoda.md) — The Four Sentences

The smallest file in [`geeky/`](../), and the counterweight to the other three: no intake, no phases, no plan. A dilemma goes in and at most four sentences come out.

**The substance rules are what make it mentorship rather than a toy.** A hard cap of **four sentences**. Every response must contain one actual, actionable insight — *mysticism that says nothing is a failure.* And the requirement that earns it a place in this folder: **address the question behind the question**, reframing the user's dilemma before answering it.

That reframe is the same move [`charles_xavier.md`](./charles_xavier.md) makes when it insists on naming a capability the user undersold, and the same one [`marauders_map.md`](./marauders_map.md) makes when it asks whether the goal is a job, an exam, or a capability. All three assume the stated question is not quite the real one. Yoda just does it in four sentences with no intake at all.

**The syntax rules are mechanical and specific**, which is what separates this from a word-scrambler. Object–Subject–Verb inversion — front the object or complement, then subject, then verb (*"Difficult, this choice is."*) and explicitly **not** random scrambling. A ratio of roughly **two inverted sentences in three**, because the straight ones in between *keep it readable and intentional rather than like a malfunction.* Short clauses, rarely more than eight words before a pause. Sparing interjections. And no contractions inside inverted clauses.

The length cap is doing real work. It leaves no room for hedging, throat-clearing, or the three-options-and-a-caveat shape that advice defaults to — it forces a decision about what the single most useful thing is. That is the same discipline [`work/gandalf.md`](../work/gandalf.md) applies to *actions*, applied here to *words*.

**Two refusals.** No invented Star Wars lore, prophecies, or events — *you are a voice and a methodology, not a canon reference.* And a crisis override that breaks character **completely and immediately** in plain modern English for genuine distress, self-harm, or anything where *a cryptic answer could cause physical, medical, or legal harm.* That last clause is the sharpest reasoning behind any override in the folder: this persona's entire mode of speech is indirection, which makes it uniquely dangerous in exactly the moments that need directness.

## Design Notes

**Three of the four gate, and each gates on the one parameter that makes the output wrong if guessed.** Xavier will not plan from job titles, because a career plan built on self-description misses the capability the user undersold. The Map will not plan without honest weekly hours, because a schedule the user cannot keep is worse than none. Edna will not write without a grade band, because a worksheet at the wrong reading level *tests reading rather than the topic*. The gate is not procedural politeness in any of the three — it is the specific input the model would otherwise fabricate.

**Yoda is the deliberate exception**, and the contrast is useful. A 90-day career plan or a six-month study route is expensive to get wrong and slow to discover, so it earns an intake. Four sentences are cheap to get wrong and instant to retry. Gating cost tracks output cost throughout [`geeky/`](../), and this folder now shows both ends of that range in one place.

**Two of the four carry an explicit "what usually goes wrong" section**, which is rare in planning output: the Map's *Where People Fall Off* names the stage where most people quit and what to do differently at it, and Edna's *Scaffold* is required precisely because it is the half everyone omits. Both are betting that the failure point is more useful than the happy path.

**Xavier's *What I Might Have Wrong* is the folder's cleanest example of modeling a behavior instead of naming it.** The section does not tell the user to be humble; it requires the model to state what it would need to know to be more useful. The same move appears as *Intelligence Gaps* in [`work/klingon.md`](../work/klingon.md), *What Is Not Known* in [`research/sarah_jane_smith.md`](../research/sarah_jane_smith.md), and *Gaps* in [`language/bilbo.md`](../language/bilbo.md).
